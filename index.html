UNDERSTAND THE MECHANISM BEFORE INTERPRETING THE DATA

What can this model help you explore?

Inspect spatial distributions, compare mechanisms and practise reading XRD. Model outputs must not be presented as experimental results.

HYDROGEN

Ingress → diffusion → trapping or precipitation

External H₂ can adsorb and dissociate at the surface. Inside the metal, hydrogen migrates as an interstitial solute, rather than as H₂ molecules travelling through the bulk. Local supersaturation and nucleation conditions influence hydride formation.

IRRADIATION

Energy transfer → displacement → defect evolution

Displacement can produce vacancies and self-interstitials. Defects may recombine, migrate to sinks such as grain boundaries, or cluster. A proton is a hydrogen nucleus and may become a source of retained H; an electron beam does not directly implant hydrogen.

PROPERTIES

Defects and phases → deformation and fracture

Defects can obstruct dislocations, while hydrides affect deformation and cracking. Orientation, size, stress and temperature also matter. Greater hardness does not guarantee greater fracture resistance. Strength, lifetime and crack growth are not calculated here.

Three reproducible exercises

  1. Follow hydrogen ingress. Select Hydrogen only, advance 500 steps and save a snapshot. Repeat with the same seed and a different temperature. Compare mobile and trapped hydrogen distributions.
  2. Explore processing history. Select Combined and advance 500 steps. Switch to heating and advance another 500 steps. Check total H conservation while trapping, dissolution and defect recovery continue.
  3. Connect structure to XRD. In the XRD explorer, change uniform strain from 0 to +0.5% and observe peak positions. Then reduce domain size separately and observe peak widths. Interpret the two effects separately.

Model rules and parameter provenance

The three modules are independent: referenced crystal geometry, an uncalibrated 2D process model, and diffraction with simplified peak profiles. Each evolution cell represents a statistical region, not an atomic site.

2D evolution rules

A 72 × 44 grid uses reflecting boundaries. Random Voronoi grains and illustrative Nb-rich regions define the starting geometry. More grains change the number of sink cells; no physical grain size is assigned.

  • Each step adds hydrogen / 9 H tracer units and radiation / 12 vacancy–interstitial pairs. Fractional source counts carry over to the next step.
  • Migration probability: m = clamp[0.32 exp{−0.12 eV / kB (1/T − 1/573.15 K)}, 0.02, 0.9]. The 0.12 eV energy and 1.4 mobility multiplier in Nb regions are teaching choices, not fitted alloy parameters.
  • Trapping depends on vacancies, grain boundaries and Nb regions, with finite capacity per cell. Detrapping probability is 0.003 + 0.045m. Complete expressions are documented in MODEL.md and engine.js.
  • Vacancies and interstitials can recombine on meeting. Grain boundaries absorb the two species separately. Elastic fields, dislocation-loop evolution and void kinetics are omitted.
  • Above a temperature-dependent mobile-H threshold in non-Nb cells, packets of 3 tracer units enter the hydride reservoir. Below the threshold, dissolution is allowed. Packet size is not chemical stoichiometry. No calibrated TSSp/TSSd curves or phase-field free energy are used.
  • Proton mode adds one H tracer for every two defect pairs. This ratio and its spatial profile are illustrative, not a real beam fluence, range or implantation efficiency.

Temperature changes preserve the current state; presets and rebuilds restart it. Mobile + trapped + precipitated H equals cumulative H input. For each defect species, remaining + recombined + sink-absorbed counts equal the number of generated pairs.

Crystal geometry and XRD equations

α-Zr: a = 0.32324 nm, c = 0.51472 nm, representative values for the initial E110 α phase in [1], not universal constants for Zr–Nb alloys. Nb: a = 0.330 nm, the BCC reference in [2]. δ hydride: a = 0.47776 nm, a specific hydrogenated specimen in [3].

HCP: 1/d² = 4(h²+hk+k²)/(3a²) + l²/c². Cubic: 1/d² = (h²+k²+l²)/a². Peak positions follow Bragg’s law. Size broadening βL = 0.9λ/(L cosθ); microstrain broadening βε = 4ε tanθ. Widths are converted to degrees and combined as β² = (0.15°)² + βL² + βε², using Gaussian profiles and a quadrature approximation.

Broadening is an isotropic teaching approximation. Real domain-size analysis requires instrumental corrections and consideration of texture, anisotropy, peak overlap and data quality. The δ view shows an FCC metal lattice with 6 of 8 tetrahedral positions occupied in one cell to illustrate ZrH₁.₅, not to predict H ordering.

Towards a calibrated research model

First confirm the alloy, initial microstructure, specimen dimensions, H concentration and processing history with your supervisor. Select a model for the question: diffusion–trapping equations for distributions, phase-field methods for hydride morphology, or validated interatomic potentials and molecular dynamics for displacement cascades. Establish D(T), trapping energies, TSSp/TSSd, defect production rates and real geometry, then validate against XRD, microscopy, H measurements and mechanical tests. Reproducibility is not physical validation.
